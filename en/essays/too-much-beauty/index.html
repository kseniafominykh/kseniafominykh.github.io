Too Much Beauty

Why creative people feel anxious right now — and why that’s normal

Introduction

You know the scene. You open Pinterest “just for a minute, for inspiration.” An hour later you have forty saved pins, a couple of other people’s works that make you ache with envy, and not a single line of your own.

There’s plenty of inspiration, yet you can’t start. You rearrange your palette, hunt for “the one” reference, save a few more images for the mood.

This isn’t laziness. It’s sabotaging the start, when the first stroke suddenly feels too heavy to lift.

The good news: you’re fine. You simply live in a moment that art has never known before.

1

There has never been this much

A Renaissance painter saw exactly what hung in nearby churches, in patrons’ homes and in their master’s workshop. The Louvre only opened to the public in 1793. Every painting by someone else was an event.

Back in 2008, researchers at the University of California, San Diego worked out how much information passes through the average American in a day — outside of work alone1. For scale: War and Peace runs to about 460,000 words. That’s nearly a quarter of Tolstoy every day. And that was the world before Instagram and TikTok.

While you were reading this paragraph, the world snapped a couple of million new references.

2

What it does to your head

Attention. Psychologist Gloria Mark has spent more than twenty years measuring how long we stay on one screen before switching: from Figma to a messenger, from the messenger to email. In the early 2000s it was about 150 seconds; by 2016–2020 it had fallen to 473. We linger on a single image in a feed for even less: according to Facebook, people look at a post on their phone for 1.7 seconds on average4. The brain gets used to that rhythm, while ideas ripen slowly.

Fixation. In 1991, David Jansson and Steven Smith gave designers a task. One group got an example solution along with it, the other didn’t. Those who had seen the example unconsciously carried its features into their own designs5. The brain clings to the first thing it sees. Now imagine having thousands of “first things” every day.

Comparison. In the feed we see other people’s best work, with no drafts and no failures. A study of artists on Instagram found that the platform genuinely inspires — but it also brings comparison and anxiety which, over time, wear down the urge to create6.

3

Hello, impostor syndrome

From there it’s a short step to “I’m not a real artist, and soon everyone will find out.” Psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes described this feeling back in 1978.

In 2020, a major review brought dozens of studies together. Across them, impostor feelings turned up in 9–82% of participants. The range is that wide because researchers used different questionnaires with different cut-offs. But even the strictest estimate means one person in eleven, and the most generous means four in five. It affects men and women alike, from teenagers to seasoned professionals7.

Clance and Imes themselves spoke of a “phenomenon”, not a “syndrome”. It isn’t a diagnosis but an experience that a great many people go through. If it feels like everyone around you is more talented, you’re in very large company.

4

We’ve been here before

Humanity has drowned in information before, more than once. In her book Too Much to Know, Harvard historian Ann Blair shows that when printing arrived, scholars of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries complained about too many books in words almost like ours8.

“The abundance of books is distraction”

Seneca, 1st century

And people found ways to live with it: notes, excerpts, alphabetical indexes, encyclopaedias. Blair is honest that today’s scale is unprecedented: there’s more information, and it has seeped into everything9. So we’re not the first to swim against the current. Ours is simply the biggest sea.

5

Anxiety isn’t a malfunction

Feeling anxious amid this turbulence doesn’t mean something is wrong with you. It’s the normal reaction of a brain that is rebuilding itself, right now, for a new volume of input. We’re adapting, just as we once adapted to the printing press. And science already offers a few hints:

  1. Boredom helps. In an experiment by Sandi Mann and Rebekah Cadman, people who spent fifteen minutes copying numbers out of a phone book went on to produce more creative ideas than those who started the task straight away. The researchers link this to daydreaming, which boredom switches on10.
  2. Knowing about the trap is half the battle. When students were told about design fixation, they copied the examples noticeably less11.
  3. More of your own ideas means less of other people’s. In the same study, the more options a team came up with, the weaker the fixation11.

But there’s no universal recipe. Some people close every tab and draw in silence. Others build a moodboard of three hundred images and only then get going. Everyone has their own way of finding ideas, and yours is fine, even if it looks nothing like the ones in other people’s reels.

The feed is endless. Your attention isn’t.

How many other people’s images you let into your head, and when you close the tab and begin — that’s yours to decide.
